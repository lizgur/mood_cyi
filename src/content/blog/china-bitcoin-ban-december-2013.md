**On 5 December 2013, the People's Bank of China (PBoC) and four other national regulators published a notice that barred China's financial institutions and payment companies from doing any bitcoin business.**[^1][^2] The notice, Yin Fa [2013] No. 289, was dated 3 December 2013.[^1] It called bitcoin "a specific virtual commodity," not money, but it did not stop ordinary people. The PBoC said individuals were free to take part at their own risk.[^1] Bitcoin had first passed $1,000 only a week earlier, and the price fell about 20% on the news.[^4] Two weeks later, the pressure moved to the payment firms that moved yuan into the exchanges, and China's biggest exchange, BTC China, stopped taking yuan deposits.[^7][^8]

## What did China's 5 December 2013 notice actually say?

The text, published by Xinhua's Economic Information Daily from the PBoC's website, has five sections.[^1] Here they are in short (our translation):

1. **What bitcoin is.** The notice lists four features of bitcoin: no central issuer, a limited total supply, no geographic limits on use, and anonymity. It says that although bitcoin is called a "currency," it is not issued by a monetary authority and has no legal-tender status, so it "is not a currency in the true sense." It "should be a specific virtual commodity" (一种特定的虚拟商品) and "cannot and should not" circulate as money.[^1]
2. **Banks and payment firms are out.** For now, financial institutions and payment institutions may not price products or services in bitcoin, buy or sell bitcoin, act as a central counterparty, or insure anything bitcoin-related. They also may not offer bitcoin registration, trading, clearing or settlement; accept bitcoin as payment; exchange it for yuan or foreign currency; store it, hold it in custody or take it as collateral; issue bitcoin-related financial products; or use it as an investment target for trusts and funds.[^1]
3. **Exchange websites must register.** Websites that offer bitcoin registration and trading must file with telecom regulators, which can shut down sites that break the law.[^1] Reuters reported this the same day as a requirement for trading platforms to register with telecommunications authorities.[^2]
4. **Anti-money-laundering rules.** Exchange sites must verify their users and require real-name registration with name and ID number. Suspicious transactions must be reported to China's anti-money-laundering monitoring centre.[^1]
5. **Public education.** Regulators and banks should teach the public what money is and warn about investment risk.[^1]

**The part that didn't ban anything.** In its statement with the notice, the PBoC said that trading bitcoin is buying and selling a commodity on the internet. Ordinary people "have the freedom to participate, on the premise that they bear the risk themselves" (在自担风险的前提下拥有参与的自由).[^1] The BBC summed it up as: banks banned, individuals "still free to trade in bitcoins."[^3]

## Why did China act in December 2013?

The notice gives its own reasons: to protect the public's property, to protect the yuan's status as legal tender, to prevent money laundering and to keep the financial system stable.[^1] It also says some organisations and individuals in China had been using bitcoin's international attention to "hype" (炒作) bitcoin and related products.[^1]

Reporters at the time added context:

- **China was at the centre of the market.** Reuters said BTC China, based in Shanghai, had recently become the world's largest bitcoin exchange by volume.[^2] CNBC, citing Bitcoinity.org, said it handled around 500,000 bitcoins the week before, about 30% of the market.[^4]
- **Capital controls.** Reuters wrote that the PBoC may have been concerned because bitcoin represented "a potential hole in the country's capital controls."[^2] The BBC said experts told Reuters that many Chinese buyers used bitcoin to get around controls on the yuan.[^3]
- **A huge run-up.** Reuters, citing bitcoinity.org, said bitcoin's dollar value had risen some 800% in the previous two months.[^2] It had [first passed $1,000 on Mt. Gox on 27 November](/blog/bitcoin-1000-dollars-2013).

Not everyone read it as hostile. On the day, Zennon Kapron of the consultancy Kapronasia told Reuters the move was "measured" and "positive," and that it "does add legitimacy" to bitcoin.[^2]

## What happened to the price?

- **5 December.** CNBC reported that bitcoin fell about 20% from around 8 a.m. London time, back below $1,000. At midday London time it was $980 on Mt. Gox and $932 on CoinDesk's index.[^4] Reuters said the yuan price on the Chinese exchange FXBTC.com fell as much as about 20% before recovering to around 5,800 yuan, and that on BTC-e the dollar price fell about 11%, from $1,063 to about $945.[^2]
- **7 December.** BBC Chinese reported a third straight day of falls. BTC China showed 4,250 yuan ($863) on Saturday afternoon, and Bitstamp was at $575, down 30%.[^5]
- **Weekend of 7–8 December.** The Global Times, citing btc123.com, said the price fell from 6,280 yuan ($1,032.43) on Thursday to about 4,669 yuan on Saturday, then rose to 4,888 yuan on Sunday.[^6]

Prices varied a lot from exchange to exchange in 2013, so treat these as snapshots, not a single "bitcoin price." This is history, not a forecast.

## Baidu and the cafés stop taking bitcoin

The notice was aimed at banks and payment firms, but Chinese businesses that took bitcoin got the message too.

- **Baidu.** Baidu Jiasule, the website-acceleration and security service owned by search giant Baidu, had started accepting bitcoin on 14 October 2013.[^5] By 7 December it said it would "suspend" bitcoin payments "from today," citing volatile prices, user protection and the government's call to control bitcoin risk.[^5][^6]
- **A Beijing café.** Cheku Cafe in Zhongguancun told the Global Times it had stopped taking bitcoin after the notice. A staff member said it had accepted bitcoin for about a month and only a few customers used it.[^6]
- **Hong Kong.** The Global Times also reported, citing chinanews.com, that the Hong Kong Monetary Authority said on Friday 6 December that bitcoin did not qualify as a means of payment or electronic money.[^6]

## 18 December 2013: cutting off the yuan

The 5 December notice didn't ban exchanges. It did, however, cover payment institutions,[^1] and Chinese exchanges depended on them. As the BBC explained, Chinese exchanges were not licensed to accept or pay out yuan themselves and relied on independent clearing firms as middlemen.[^8]

- **16 December (Monday).** According to Xinhua, as carried by China Economic Net, the PBoC met with more than ten third-party payment companies. It told them not to provide payment and clearing services to bitcoin, litecoin and similar trading websites, and to end existing partnerships. Balances already held could be withdrawn before the Spring Festival, but there could be no new payments.[^7] The BBC, citing Yicai, described it as a meeting with 10 clearing houses, which were given until the end of January to cut their links to the exchanges.[^8]
- **18 December (Wednesday).** BTC China announced it had stopped taking yuan deposits and ended its deposit services with the payment companies YeePay (易宝支付) and Tenpay (财付通). Yuan and bitcoin withdrawals still worked.[^7] CEO Bobby Lee told the South China Morning Post, as quoted by the BBC: "We're still operating a Bitcoin exchange in China legally, and we're still allowing people to deposit and withdraw Bitcoin, and withdraw renminbi."[^8]
- **The price.** The BBC reported BTC China as low as 2,560 yuan ($421), citing the SCMP. That compared with a high of 7,588 yuan ($1,250) in late November. On Mt. Gox the price fell from $717 to as low as $480 that day.[^8] Xinhua put bitcoin at around 2,800 yuan at 3 p.m. on 18 December, down from more than 6,000 yuan at the start of the month.[^7]

## What came later: 2017 and 2021

The 2013 notice was the first round, not the last. Briefly:

- **4 September 2017: ICOs banned.** The PBoC and six other agencies ordered all initial coin offerings to stop immediately. They called ICOs unapproved, illegal public fundraising. The same announcement barred token trading platforms from exchanging yuan for tokens or "virtual currencies," and again barred banks and non-bank payment firms from related services.[^9]
- **14–30 September 2017: BTC China closes its exchange.** BTC China (by then BTCC) said it would stop all trading on 30 September 2017, citing the regulators' directive. Its mining pool was not affected.[^10]
- **24 September 2021: "illegal financial activities."** Ten agencies, including the PBoC, China's supreme court and prosecutors, and the police ministry, published Yin Fa [2021] No. 237. It declared that virtual-currency business, including fiat-to-crypto exchange, crypto-to-crypto exchange, central-counterparty trading and crypto derivatives, is illegal financial activity. It said overseas exchanges serving people in China were illegal too. The notice is dated 15 September 2021.[^11]

So the wording of 2013 kept coming back. In 2013 bitcoin was "not a currency in the true sense" but "a virtual commodity." In 2017 tokens and "virtual currencies" had no legal status equal to money, and in 2021 none equal to legal tender. The ban on banks and payment firms appears in all three.[^1][^9][^11]

## Why the 2013 ban matters

- **It was a major central bank ruling on what bitcoin is.** The PBoC called it a commodity, not money.[^1] That was the starting point for China's later rules.[^9][^11]
- **It showed where the pressure point was.** Regulators didn't need to ban bitcoin to make trading harder. They went after the banks and payment rails that moved yuan in and out.[^1][^7][^8]
- **It fits bitcoin's origin story.** Bitcoin's first block carries the Times headline "Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks."[^12] Five years later, a central bank was ruling that banks must stay away from it.
