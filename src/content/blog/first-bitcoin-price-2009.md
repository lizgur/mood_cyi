**On 5 October 2009, a pseudonymous early Bitcoiner calling themselves NewLibertyStandard published the first known dollar exchange rate for bitcoin: $1.00 = 1,309.03 BTC — about $0.00076 per coin.** The number came from the cost of electricity to mine, not from an order book. Seven days later, Martti Malmi sold 5,050 BTC for $5.02 via PayPal, the first known bitcoin-for-fiat trade. Pizza Day was still 229 days away.

That first posted rate is how bitcoin stopped being only a protocol experiment and started having a price someone would quote in dollars.

## Who was NewLibertyStandard?

NewLibertyStandard was a forum regular who ran a small website offering to buy and sell bitcoins for US dollars. There was no matching engine and no account system. You emailed how many coins you wanted to trade, paid or received money through PayPal, and NewLibertyStandard moved the coins by hand.

Their real-world identity has never been publicly revealed. They later registered as Bitcointalk user #26, helped test early software (Satoshi thanked them in the Bitcoin 0.2 release notes for Linux testing), and on 5 February 2010 proposed adopting the Thai baht symbol ฿ and the three-letter code **BTC** — the ticker that stuck.

## How the rate was calculated

NewLibertyStandard's own words, preserved on their Exchange Rate page (archived by the Wayback Machine and mirrored in later rate tables):

> During 2009 my exchange rate was calculated by dividing $1.00 by the average amount of electricity required to run a computer with high CPU for a year, 1331.5 kWh, multiplied by the the average residential cost of electricity in the United States for the previous year, $0.1136, divided by 12 months divided by the number of bitcoins generated by my computer over the past 30 days.

In plain English: take the yearly electricity bill for a hard-working CPU, turn it into a monthly cost, then divide by how many coins that machine had mined in the last month. The result was a cost-of-production price, not a market-clearing price from many buyers and sellers.

The archived mid-market history lists **$1.00 USD = 1,309.03 BTC** on **10/05/2009** as the first entry, then moves day by day through the rest of 2009 as mining output and the formula shifted the quote.

## The first bitcoin-for-dollars trade

Publishing a rate is one thing. Someone actually swapping coins for dollars is another.

On **12 October 2009**, Finnish developer Martti Malmi (Sirius), who worked closely with Satoshi on early Bitcoin software, sold **5,050 BTC for $5.02** to NewLibertyStandard via PayPal. Malmi later said he found the trade in his email backups and that he sold the coins to help get the world's first bitcoin trading service started. Reuters' ten-year Bitcoin factbox records the same sale and the New Liberty Standard valuation of 1,309.03 bitcoins to one dollar.

That trade sits on-chain as well: explorers flag transaction `7dff938918f07619abd38e4510890396b1cef4fbeca154fb7aafba8843295ea2` in block 24,835 as the first known sale of bitcoin for USD.

A week earlier the posted rate was still cost-based. A week later there was proof someone would move real dollars for real coins.

## Before Pizza Day — and before the markets most people remember

Put the first price on the early timeline:

- 31 Oct 2008: the [whitepaper](/blog/bitcoin-whitepaper-explained)
- 3 Jan 2009: the genesis block
- 12 Jan 2009: [block 170, Satoshi sends Hal Finney 10 BTC](/blog/hal-finney-first-bitcoin-transaction)
- **5 Oct 2009: NewLibertyStandard posts $1 = 1,309.03 BTC**
- 12 Oct 2009: Malmi sells 5,050 BTC for $5.02
- 22 May 2010: [Pizza Day](/blog/bitcoin-pizza-day-transaction) — [Laszlo Hanyecz](/blog/laszlo-hanyecz) pays 10,000 BTC for two pizzas
- 18 Jul 2010: Mt. Gox opens (after Bitcoin Market earlier that year)
- **15 Aug 2010: the [value overflow incident](/blog/bitcoin-value-overflow-incident-2010)**

At the 5 October rate, 10,000 BTC was about **$7.64**. By Pizza Day, the community was treating 10,000 coins as worth enough to buy dinner — the familiar "~$41" figure for those pizzas is a later forum estimate of the food cost, not NewLibertyStandard's quote. Either way, price discovery had already started seven months earlier, with electricity as the yardstick.

Later venues such as Bitcoin Market (early 2010) and Mt. Gox (July 2010) let buyers and sellers set a floating price. NewLibertyStandard came first: one counterparty, a posted production-cost rate, and PayPal.

The full stretch of those years is in our [Bitcoin history timeline 2008–2010](/blog/bitcoin-history-timeline-2008-2010). 5 October belongs with the other dates on our [Bitcoin anniversaries calendar](/blog/bitcoin-anniversaries-calendar).

## Why a cost-of-production price mattered

In late 2009 almost nobody "needed" bitcoin. Mining was the main way to get coins. Pegging a dollar figure to the power bill made a simple claim: these coins cost something real to create, so they can be exchanged for something real.

Satoshi liked the approach. In correspondence with Martti Malmi around that time he praised estimating value from electricity — a cost-anchored idea that still shows up whenever people talk about mining and security budget.

It was also fragile. One person's machine, one country's power price, and a 30-day window. As more miners joined, that personal output fell and the formula pushed the quoted dollar price up. Forum users argued about whether production cost equals market price. Those arguments helped push the community toward real markets with bids and asks.
