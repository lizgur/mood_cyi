**The first bitcoin ATM opened to the public on Tuesday 29 October 2013, inside a Waves Coffee House at Howe and Smithe streets in downtown Vancouver.**[^1] It was a Robocoin machine, built by a Nevada start-up and run by Bitcoiniacs, a local bitcoin store.[^1][^2] It worked in both directions: you could put Canadian cash in and get bitcoin, or send bitcoin and get cash out. A palm scanner kept each user under a C$3,000-a-day limit.[^1][^3][^4] Wired reported that the machine was working, and had its first customer, on the evening of Monday 28 October, the night before the official opening.[^3] Wired called it the first operational bitcoin ATM "as far as we know." CoinDesk later called it the first bitcoin ATM "to be used commercially."[^2][^7]

## When and where was the first bitcoin ATM?

The machine was installed at Waves Coffee, "right next to the provincial courthouse in downtown Vancouver," on Monday 28 October 2013.[^2] CBC gives the address as Howe and Smithe streets.[^1] Wired watched Mitchell Demeter and his business partner Jackson Warren spend about an hour that morning unpacking the 750-pound machine. They briefly thought it had been shipped without a power cord, until they found the cord inside.[^2]

**Two dates, both correct.** CBC reported that the kiosk "went live at 9 a.m." on Tuesday 29 October, which is the official opening day.[^1] Wired's report on 29 October said the ATM "went live last night," after an afternoon of testing on the Monday, and that its first customer used it that evening.[^3] A CoinDesk article a month later gives the launch date as 30 October, but CoinDesk's own launch-day report was published on 30 October from an event that had already happened, so 29 October is the date to use.[^4][^6]

**Was it really the first?** Robocoin had tested a machine at a convention in San Jose, California, in May 2013, but Wired says Vancouver was "the first time they've installed one of their machines in a permanent fashion."[^3] Robocoin's rival Lamassu was already taking pre-orders for a $5,000 machine in August 2013.[^5] That's why the careful wording is "first public" or "first commercially used" bitcoin ATM, which is how Wired and CoinDesk put it.[^2][^7]

## Who was behind it?

- **Bitcoiniacs (the operator).** Wired described its owners as three men who met in high school and founded a bitcoin trading store in Vancouver.[^3] CoinDesk said the store opened at the end of June 2013, and that founder Mitchell Demeter had started out trading on LocalBitcoins.[^5] On launch day it had a storefront on First Avenue.[^4] "The point of the store was to make it easier for everyone to buy bitcoins," co-founder Jackson Warren told Wired. "We saw the ATM as a way to expand the model of the store."[^3]
- **Robocoin (the maker).** Robocoin's CEO was Jordan Kelley. His model was "selling hardware to operators" and supplying the software and know-your-customer (KYC) compliance.[^4]
- **The order.** In September 2013 CoinDesk reported that Bitcoiniacs had bought five machines at $18,500 each. The plan was Vancouver first, then Montréal, Ottawa, Toronto and Calgary by early December.[^5] Wired put the partners' total outlay at "more than $90,000."[^2]

## How did the Robocoin ATM work?

**Cash only, both ways.** It didn't take debit or credit cards. You fed in cash and it sent bitcoin to an address, or it scanned a QR code on your phone and paid out cash for bitcoin.[^3] CBC adds that users could also choose a paper voucher for the transaction.[^1] Kelley called it "the first bidirectional bitcoin ATM."[^4]

**A palm scanner instead of a card.** The machine scanned your palm and matched it against a database so that nobody could trade more than C$3,000 a day, which Wired described as a way of deterring money launderers.[^3] Kelley's pitch: "Walk up to the machine, tap buy or sell, scan your hand to create a unique ID, but with anonymity."[^4]

**ID: the plan and the reality.** In September, Kelley described a four-step sign-up: a photo, a palm-vein scan, an ID scan and an email address.[^5] By launch, Wired reported that "at least in Canada, you don't need any identification," apart from the palm scan, and that a US version would have stricter ID requirements.[^2][^3]

**Why C$3,000?** Demeter told CoinDesk he was keeping transactions under $3,000 because above that the Canadian regulator, FINTRAC, required more extensive record-keeping.[^5] A FINTRAC spokesman told Wired that a simple bitcoin-for-cash trade wasn't money transmission in Canada, and that "just operating a private-label ATM isn't subject to the requirements."[^3] In the US, Wired noted, most states required a money transmission licence, which is why the machine made its debut in Canada.[^2][^3]

**The exchange behind it.** Every trade was placed on an online exchange in real time.[^3] In September the plan was an account at Calgary-based VirtEx, and CBC still named VirtEx on launch day.[^5][^1] Wired and CoinDesk, reporting from the launch, both said the machine was using Bitstamp.[^3][^4] A year later CoinDesk wrote that Robocoin machines required operators to use Bitstamp.[^7]

## Launch day: a first customer and a queue

Getting it running took most of Monday. During testing, the Bitstamp connection went down for a few minutes, the ATM's Windows software booted into safe mode, the receipt printer didn't work at first, and a plastic cover had to come off the palm reader. Wired put the tweaking at about seven hours.[^3]

According to Wired, a man named Jason Lamarche turned up at about 6 p.m. on Monday and waited more than three hours to be the first to use it. He put in a $20 bill and in less than a minute had a tenth of a bitcoin, which he spent at the coffee bar on a steamed milk with vanilla. "It was super convenient. It was easy to use," he told Wired.[^3]

On the Tuesday, CBC reported that the machine drew its first customers and dozens of onlookers shortly after the 9 a.m. opening.[^1] Kelley, speaking to CoinDesk from the launch: "There's a line in front of the machine right now."[^4]

**Price that morning.** CBC reported that Bitcoiniacs was buying bitcoin at $183.21 and selling at $207.37 to $211.32. CBC added that the price had started 2013 at $13 and peaked at $266 in April.[^1] For where bitcoin's price story began, see [the first Bitcoin price in 2009](/blog/first-bitcoin-price-2009).

## The first month

CoinDesk reported on 27 November 2013, citing Robocoin, that the Vancouver machine had taken more than CA$1 million in 29 days across more than 1,500 transactions. The same report gave these company figures:[^6]

- **Day one:** CA$10,000 in 81 transactions.
- **First week:** CA$81,000, beating Robocoin's first-month goal of CA$50,000.
- **Day 17:** the machine had broken even for its operators.
- **New users:** according to the company, more than half of purchases created a new wallet.

These are Robocoin's numbers, not audited ones. CoinDesk later repeated the CA$1 million and 17-day figures and wrote: "The bitcoin ATM gold-rush had begun."[^7]

## What happened to Robocoin and Bitcoiniacs?

- **Problems.** In January 2014, CoinDesk reported, a problem with Bitstamp, the only exchange the machine could use, left "dozens of angry customers with unprocessed orders" at the Waves machine.[^7]
- **Competition.** By September 2014 CoinDesk described Lamassu as the maker with the largest market share. It sold compact buy-only machines for $6,500, while Robocoin's large two-way machines cost about $15,000 and charged operators 1% per transaction.[^7]
- **Robocoin becomes Romit.** In April 2015 Robocoin launched Romit, a cash remittance service. TechCrunch reported that Robocoin then ran 20 ATMs around the world.[^8]
- **The end of Robocoin's bitcoin business.** In January 2016 Romit told customers it was discontinuing bitcoin services and asked them to withdraw their bitcoin, with services to end on 15 February 2016. CoinDesk noted that Robocoin's early lead had eroded amid operator complaints about hardware and software problems and allegations that refunds were being withheld.[^9]
- **Bitcoiniacs.** In September 2014 CoinDesk still described Bitcoiniacs as the operator of the first ATM at Waves. Demeter said then: "For a lot of people we're the first contact they have with bitcoin."[^7] Today a business trading as Bitcoiniacs lists bitcoin ATMs around Vancouver, including one at Waves Coffee, 900 Howe St (list dated June 2026). It says it has operated in Vancouver since 2014, so we don't treat it as the same machine as the 2013 Robocoin.[^10]

## How many bitcoin ATMs are there now?

When we checked on 22 November 2026, Coin ATM Radar's homepage listed **27,024 crypto ATMs in 57 countries**, from 43 producers and 288 operators.[^11] That count covers all crypto ATMs, not just bitcoin-only machines, and Coin ATM Radar updates it continuously, so expect it to have changed by the time you read this.

## Why the first bitcoin ATM matters

- **It made bitcoin physical.** Before ATMs, getting bitcoin usually meant an online exchange account, which, in Kelley's words, was "arduous."[^4] Online exchanges started in 2010, as we covered in [the first Bitcoin exchanges](/blog/first-bitcoin-exchange-2010).
- **It showed compliance would shape the hardware.** The palm scanner, the C$3,000 cap and the choice of Canada were all responses to regulation.[^3][^5]
- **It showed the weak link.** The machine relied on a single back-end exchange, and when that exchange had problems, so did the customers.[^3][^7] Bitcoin had been through a different kind of reliability scare that spring: [the March 2013 chain fork](/blog/bitcoin-chain-fork-march-2013).
