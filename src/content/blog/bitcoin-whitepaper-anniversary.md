On **Friday, 31 October 2008, at 18:10 UTC**, an unknown author using the name **Satoshi Nakamoto** sent an email titled *"Bitcoin P2P e-cash paper"* to the Cryptography Mailing List hosted at metzdowd.com. It opened with one line: *"I've been working on a new electronic cash system that's fully peer-to-peer, with no trusted third party."* Linked in the email was a nine-page paper: [**Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System**](https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf).

On 31 October 2026, that paper turns 18. Here is the story of the Whitepaper, the short document that started Bitcoin.

## Halloween 2008: a quiet email in a loud year

The timing matters. In autumn 2008 the financial system was in crisis. Lehman Brothers had collapsed in September, and governments were rushing out bank bailouts. Into that noise, Satoshi posted a proposal for money that needs no bank to work.

The email went to a small list read by cryptographers and cypherpunks, people who had spent years trying to build digital cash. Earlier attempts such as Adam Back's Hashcash, Wei Dai's b-money and Nick Szabo's bit gold had each solved part of the problem. The Whitepaper put the pieces together.

You can read the [31 October 2008 announcement](https://satoshi.nakamotoinstitute.org/emails/cryptography/) in the Satoshi Nakamoto Institute archive.

## What the nine pages say

The Whitepaper is short: about nine pages, 12 sections and 8 references. Its central problem is **double-spending**. Digital files can be copied, so what stops someone from spending the same coin twice without a bank keeping the books?

Satoshi's answer combined a few ideas:

- **A public chain of blocks.** Transactions are grouped into blocks, each linked to the one before by a cryptographic hash.
- **Proof-of-work.** Adding a block takes real computing effort, so rewriting history would mean out-working the whole honest network.
- **The longest chain wins.** Every node follows the valid chain with the most work behind it.
- **Incentives.** Whoever adds a block earns new coins and fees, which rewards honest participation.

## The first replies: doubt, then curiosity

The list was not instantly convinced. Early replies questioned whether the system could scale. On 7 November 2008, **Hal Finney** replied with one of the first constructive responses, calling Bitcoin "a very promising idea" and asking for more detail on how nodes would follow the rules. A copy of that reply is in the [mail-archive.com Cryptography Mailing List archive](https://www.mail-archive.com/cryptography@metzdowd.com/msg09975.html).

## From paper to network

The Whitepaper became working software within weeks:

- **3 January 2009:** Satoshi mined the **genesis block**, the first block of Bitcoin, with a newspaper headline embedded in it.
- **9 January 2009:** Bitcoin v0.1 was released.
- **12 January 2009:** Satoshi sent bitcoin to Hal Finney in block 170, the first person-to-person Bitcoin transaction.
- **22 May 2010:** Laszlo Hanyecz paid 10,000 BTC for two pizzas, the first well-known purchase of real goods with bitcoin.

Satoshi stepped away around 2010 to 2011. Their identity is still unknown. The design in the Whitepaper is still how Bitcoin works today.
