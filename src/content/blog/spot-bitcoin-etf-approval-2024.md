**The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission approved the first spot bitcoin exchange-traded products on Wednesday, 10 January 2024. One order, Release No. 34-99306, cleared 11 of them at once, listed on NYSE Arca, Nasdaq and Cboe BZX.**[^1][^2] SEC Chair Gary Gensler said: "Today, the Commission approved the listing and trading of a number of spot bitcoin exchange-traded product (ETP) shares."[^1] The vote was 3-2.[^6] Trading started the next morning, Thursday 11 January.[^14][^15] The day before the approval, someone hijacked the SEC's own X account and announced it early, which turned out to be fake.[^10][^12] Between 2018 and March 2023 the SEC had turned down more than 20 of these filings. A court ruling in Grayscale's favour in August 2023 is what changed things.[^1][^7]

## What is a spot bitcoin ETF?

It's a fund that holds actual bitcoin and whose shares trade on a stock exchange like any other share. "Spot" means the fund holds the coins themselves, not futures contracts on them. The SEC calls these exchange-traded products, or ETPs. The press mostly says ETFs, and we use both here.[^1][^16]

Before January 2024, U.S. investors who wanted a fund on an exchange could only buy bitcoin futures ETFs, which hold futures contracts instead of coins.[^16] Futures had been around since December 2017, when Cboe and CME launched them (see [the first bitcoin futures](/blog/first-bitcoin-futures-cboe-cme-2017)). Commissioner Hester Peirce pointed out that bitcoin futures ETFs started trading in 2021, and that the SEC approved bitcoin futures products under the 1933 Act in 2022.[^4]

The approved trusts were set up for **cash** creation and redemption only. That means the big firms that create and redeem fund shares (authorized participants) did it with dollars, not bitcoin. The SEC said in-kind creation was outside the scope of the order.[^2]

## A decade of "no": 2013 to 2023

Peirce wrote that approval came "more than ten years after the filing of the first spot bitcoin exchange-traded product ('ETP') application," and that it took a "DC Circuit-ex-machina" to get there.[^4] Here are the main steps:

- **10 March 2017: Winklevoss Bitcoin Trust disapproved.** Bats BZX Exchange had filed to list the trust in June 2016.[^8] The SEC said an exchange listing a commodity-trust ETP needs surveillance-sharing agreements with significant markets for the commodity, and that those markets must be regulated. Its finding: "the significant markets for bitcoin are unregulated."[^8]
- **26 July 2018: Winklevoss disapproved again.** The Commission set aside a staff decision and disapproved the trust a second time (Release No. 34-83723).[^2][^7] Peirce dissented. She said the rule change met the legal standard, and that the SEC's approach "sends a strong signal that innovation is unwelcome in our markets."[^9]
- **2018 to March 2023: more than 20 disapprovals.** That's Gensler's own count, starting under Chair Jay Clayton in 2018.[^1] The D.C. Circuit listed more examples, including the United States Bitcoin and Treasury Investment Trust in 2020, and NYDIG, One River and Bitwise in 2022.[^7] Each time the reason was the same: the SEC wanted a surveillance-sharing agreement with a regulated market "of significant size," and no proposal passed that test.[^7]
- **April and May 2022: futures funds approved.** Meanwhile the SEC approved two bitcoin futures ETPs, Teucrium and Valkyrie XBTO, because their exchanges' surveillance-sharing agreements with CME satisfied it.[^7]
- **2022: Grayscale denied.** The SEC turned down NYSE Arca's filing to convert the Grayscale Bitcoin Trust into an ETP (the order was published in the Federal Register on 6 July 2022). Grayscale took it to court.[^7]

## 29 August 2023: Grayscale v. SEC

On 29 August 2023, a three-judge panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit (Chief Judge Srinivasan, Judge Rao and Senior Judge Edwards) ruled for Grayscale. Judge Rao wrote the opinion.[^7] It opens: "It is a fundamental principle of administrative law that agencies must treat like cases alike."[^7]

The court's point was simple. The SEC had approved two bitcoin **futures** funds but rejected Grayscale's **spot** fund, even though both tracked the same price and their exchanges had the same surveillance-sharing agreements with CME.[^7] Grayscale had presented "uncontested evidence" of a 99.9% correlation between bitcoin's spot price and CME futures prices.[^7] The court held: "The denial of Grayscale's proposal was arbitrary and capricious because the Commission failed to explain its different treatment of similar products," and it vacated the order.[^7]

At the time, according to the court, the Grayscale trust held 3.4% of all outstanding bitcoin. Its shares traded only over the counter, often at a discount to the coins it held.[^7]

Gensler later said this was the reason for the change. "Circumstances, however, have changed," he wrote, and approving the funds was "the most sustainable path forward."[^1]

## 9 January 2024: the fake approval from @SECGov

The day before the real decision, the SEC's own X account announced it, and the announcement was fake.

- **4:11 p.m. ET:** shortly after 4 p.m., an unauthorized party took over @SECGov by getting control of the phone number linked to the account. They posted what claimed to be the SEC's approval of spot bitcoin ETFs, then a second post that said "$BTC."[^10] According to the indictment, the fake post said in part: "Today the SEC grants approval for #Bitcoin ETFs for listing on all registered national securities exchanges."[^12]
- **4:26 p.m. ET:** SEC staff posted on Gensler's official account that @SECGov had been compromised and that the Commission had not approved anything.[^10]
- **4:42 p.m. ET:** a new post from @SECGov said the account had been compromised.[^10] The SEC's spokesperson added that "The unauthorized tweet regarding bitcoin ETFs was not made by the SEC or its staff."[^11]

**How it happened.** The SEC later said it was a "SIM swap": someone got the carrier to move the SEC's phone number to their own SIM card, then reset the account password. The SEC also admitted that multi-factor authentication had been switched off on the account since July 2023.[^11] Gensler pointed out that the Commission announces its actions on sec.gov, not on social media.[^10]

**The price swing.** Prosecutors said bitcoin rose more than $1,000 right after the fake post, then fell more than $2,000 once the SEC corrected it.[^12][^13] An Alabama man, Eric Council Jr., pleaded guilty to conspiracy to commit aggravated identity theft and access device fraud for carrying out the SIM swap. He was sentenced on 16 May 2025 to 14 months in prison and three years of supervised release.[^13]

## 10 January 2024: the real approval

The real order went out the next day, on sec.gov. It approved 11 exchange rule filings "on an accelerated basis."[^2]

**How the SEC got to yes.** In its earlier rejections, the SEC couldn't find that CME's bitcoin futures market was a market "of significant size" related to spot bitcoin, and it still didn't say so this time. It used an alternative route. The SEC ran its own correlation analysis, which found CME bitcoin futures were "consistently highly correlated" with a group of spot bitcoin platforms over the past 2.5 years: no less than 98.4% with hourly data, 94.2% at five-minute intervals and 76.9% at one-minute intervals.[^2] From that, it concluded that fraud or manipulation affecting spot prices "would likely similarly impact CME bitcoin futures prices." That meant the exchanges' surveillance-sharing agreements with CME could help catch it.[^2]

So the CME futures market that opened in December 2017 ended up being the reason the spot funds got approved (more on that market in [our futures post](/blog/first-bitcoin-futures-cboe-cme-2017)).

**What the commissioners said:**

- **Gensler (for):** the approval was "cabined to ETPs holding one non-security commodity, bitcoin," and "we did not approve or endorse bitcoin." He called bitcoin "primarily a speculative, volatile asset."[^1]
- **Peirce (for):** titled her statement "Out, Damned Spot! Out, I Say!" and called the long run of denials "an unnecessary, but consequential, saga."[^4]
- **Uyeda (for):** agreed with the approval but had "strong concerns" with the reasoning. He said the order was designed partly "to prevent a first-mover advantage among spot bitcoin ETPs."[^5]
- **Crenshaw (against):** called the actions "unsound and ahistorical" and said they "put us on a wayward path that could further sacrifice investor protection."[^3]
- **Lizárraga (against):** voted no. A law-firm summary noted he had not published a statement when it wrote up the vote.[^6]

## 11 January 2024: the first day of trading

Cboe's new-issue notices put the first trading date for its six funds at Thursday 11 January 2024, opening with a new-issue auction at about 9:30 a.m. ET.[^14] Investopedia's launch-day piece said all 11 approved funds would trade from that day across the three exchanges.[^15]

What the reports said about day one (their figures, not ours):

- **Volume:** about $4.6 billion traded across the new funds on day one, per LSEG data as reported by Zacks. That included about $2.3 billion in Grayscale's GBTC, about $1 billion in iShares' IBIT and $685 million in Fidelity's FBTC.[^17]
- **Price:** CNBC reported that bitcoin briefly topped $49,000 as the funds began trading, its highest level since December 2021.[^16]
- **Caution:** the same CNBC piece quoted Morningstar's Bryan Armour: "Fear of missing out is a poor investment strategy."[^16]

We're not tracking flows or prices since then. This post is about how the approval happened.

## Why 10 January 2024 matters in Bitcoin history

- **It ended a ten-year argument.** The SEC said "significant markets for bitcoin are unregulated" in 2017 and approved 11 spot funds in 2024, both under the same Exchange Act standard.[^8][^2]
- **A court forced it.** The SEC didn't change its mind on its own. Gensler said so himself: after the D.C. Circuit ruling, approval was the "most sustainable path forward."[^1][^7]
- **It was regulators approving products, not approving bitcoin.** Gensler: "we did not approve or endorse bitcoin."[^1]
- **It's a long way from the original idea.** Satoshi's whitepaper describes "a purely peer-to-peer version of electronic cash" that lets payments go "directly from one party to another without going through a financial institution."[^18] About fifteen years later, some of Wall Street's best-known asset managers were offering bitcoin in a fund wrapper on regulated exchanges.[^2]
